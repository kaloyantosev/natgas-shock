Natural Gas Shock Monitor

Working paper · Commodities & equity risk

Natural gas: a hedge and an early warning for stocks?

The idea has two parts. As a leading indicator, natural gas spikes in the days and weeks before the S&P 500 breaks. As a hedge, it moves against the trend: when stocks fall, gas goes up. This paper tests both on every major decline since 2001.

Abstract

1 · The two roles

What each role would look like

Leading indicator

Gas spikes before stocks fall

A sharp burst up in gas, a 10%+ gain within five trading days, somewhere in the week to two months before the S&P 500 peaks. It is the spike that matters, not where gas ends the month: a burst that fades still counts.

Hedge

Gas rises while stocks fall

From the S&P 500's peak to its low, gas goes the other way. Holding gas would then offset part of the loss on stocks, the way gold or Treasuries sometimes do.

Methods

Definitions
  1. Major decline = S&P 500 close at least 15% below a closing high (20%+ = bear market). An episode ends at a new high or after a 25% rally off its low, so the 2010 and 2011 declines, which happened below the 2007 high, count separately. Windows are measured back from the peak, the last close before the decline.
  2. Spike = a gain of 10% or more in Henry Hub front-month gas (NG=F) over 5 trading days, ending anywhere in the window. About one 5-day period in fourteen qualifies. Prices are roll-adjusted: the day the series switches contract is dropped, because the gap between two contracts is not a price move.
  3. Hedge = gas move over the same days as the S&P 500's fall from peak to low, against random windows of the same lengths; and gas over every 21-day window in which the S&P fell 5% or more.
  4. Real-time test uses fixed buckets (5/10/15/20%) and a 6-month horizon, so nothing from the future goes into it.
  5. p-values come from circular shifts of the event dates, which keep clustering and overlapping windows intact; correlations from permutation tests.
Limitations
  • Seven declines is a very small sample. Several statistics are tested here, so any single p-value near 0.05 is weak evidence.
  • Peaks are dated with hindsight. Only the real-time test reflects what an investor could have known.
  • One episode dominates the hedge: in 2022 gas more than doubled while stocks fell, driven by the war in Ukraine. Averages that include it look far better than the typical decline.
  • The sample starts in 2001, so the 2000–2002 bear market is not covered. Henry Hub is a US price; European gas (TTF) is not covered.
